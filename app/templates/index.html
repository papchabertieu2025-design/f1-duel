Grid or model?

Our question: before a Formula 1 race starts, can machine learning predict the finishing order better than the starting grid? And on which tracks should we simply trust the grid?

Model B: the grid

No learning at all: we predict that drivers finish in the order they start. It is the simple rule every fan knows, and the baseline our professor asked us to beat.

Model A: our machine-learning model

A softmax ranking model (the multiclass logistic regression of the course, applied to a whole race). It also knows each driver's pace in qualifying and practice, and how strong his car is.

Step 2 of 9

How we judge a forecast, without cheating

Why we do this

Model A and model B both give an order of the drivers. We need one fair number that says how close an order is to the real result, and we must test the model only on races it has never seen.

What we do

We count the pairs of drivers put in the right order. For every two drivers, the forecast is right if it puts ahead the one who really finished ahead. A race with 22 drivers has 231 pairs, so the score uses the whole result, not only the winner.

A tiny example with three drivers

Forecast: Norris, Russell, Sainz. Real result: Russell, Norris, Sainz.

PairForecast saysReally happenedRight?
Norris – RussellNorris aheadRussell ahead✘
Norris – SainzNorris aheadNorris ahead✔
Russell – SainzRussell aheadRussell ahead✔

The forecast is right on 2 pairs out of 3 = 67%.

50% is a coin flip; 100% is the perfect order. We also count the points scorers found: how many of the 10 drivers a model ranks highest really finish in the top 10.

Test 2: each race of 2026 is predicted by a model re-trained on all earlier races (20 races).

What it means

In the labs we split the data at random (train_test_split). Here we cannot: races follow each other in time, and a random split would let the model learn from 2023 to predict 2022. Splitting by season keeps the test honest: when we say “model A gets 76.8%”, it is on races it had never seen.

Step 3 of 9 · unsupervised learning

Car families: PCA, then k-means

Why we do this

Our professor noted that “some teams simply have better cars”. We have seven statistics per team, but they mostly say the same thing. We want a simple description of a car that the model can use.

What we do

Exactly the two tools of the PCA / k-means lab. First we standardise the seven statistics (they have different units: points, positions, %). PCA then tells us how many independent directions they really contain. k-means then groups the cars into families.

What it means

Seven statistics are really two ideas: how fast the car is and how reliable it is. So we can describe each car with just two numbers that anyone understands: its points per race (performance) and its mechanical-failure rate (reliability). PCA does not use the race results at all; it only looks at how the statistics move together.

Step 4 of 9 · supervised learning

Model A: one score per driver, then a simulated race

Why this model

Logistic regression (sessions 6–7) answers a yes/no question. A race is not yes/no: it is an order of 22 drivers. Softmax, the multiclass version seen in class, can pick the winner among many; applied again and again (winner, then second, then third…), it gives a whole order and probabilities.

What we do
  • Each driver gets a score s: a weighted sum of six standardised features.
  • The chance to win is the softmax of the scores.
  • The six weights are learned from past races by minimising the cross-entropy plus a Ridge penalty (λ). We coded the gradient by hand.
  • Before each race, we simulate it 10,000 times, with retirements, and count.

The formula and its six weights

score = Σ weight × (value − mean) / SDP(win) = escore / Σ escoreP(X ahead of Y) = 1 / (1 + e−(score X − score Y))

What it means

Model A does not ignore the grid: the grid is one of its inputs. But it can correct the grid when a fast car starts at the back (a bad qualifying, a penalty) or when a slow car starts too high. That is where it can beat model B. And because the model is only six weights, we can explain every forecast, as above.

Step 5 of 9 · model comparison

Check: would another model of the course do better?

Why we do this

We chose model A. But maybe a tree, a random forest, KNN, a kernel method or a neural network predicts races better? To be sure, we give the same data and the same test to ten other models from the course. They are a check; they are not used for the forecasts.

What we do

As in the labs: for each model we tune its main setting (tree depth, number of neighbours k, number of trees, penalty λ…), compare the score on the training data with the score on hidden data, keep the best setting, then put all the models in one summary table.

What it means

The information matters more than the algorithm. Simple linear models with 13 numbers do as well as a forest with thousands of nodes: once a model knows the grid, the pace and the team's strength, extra flexibility adds variance but removes little bias. So we keep model A: it is as good as the others, it has only six weights that we can read, and it gives probabilities (to win, to score, to beat a rival), which a single predicted position cannot give.

Step 6 of 9 · interpretation

What matters beyond the grid?

Why we do this

Our professor suggested: “a random forest with feature importance might show naturally from the data” which information counts. We also want to check that model A's six features are the right ones.

What we do

Permutation importance. We take the random forest of step 5. On the unseen seasons 2020–2025, we shuffle one feature among the drivers of each race (so it becomes useless) and measure how many pairs the forest now gets wrong. A big loss = an important feature. We also draw a small decision tree, because its rules can be read.

Result

What it means

After the grid, what helps is the qualifying gap (how fast the driver really was, not only his position) and the strength of the team. The other statistics are redundant once these are known, exactly what PCA said in step 3. This confirms model A's short list of features: grid, the two lap gaps, team points and the car family.

What we cannot claim, and what comes next

Small samples

Twenty races in 2026 and about one race per track per year. The choice for Madrid rests on a single race.

What nobody knows before the start

Crashes, safety cars and rain decide many races. No model can know them on Saturday, which limits every forecast.
